# The tuning

<EditorialMark /> This chapter's claim, and it is ours to carry: **the New Standard Tuning
reads as a harmonic theory implemented in hardware** — a preference for open, stackable
intervals built into the instrument before a note is chosen. Hold the claim loosely; the
evidence is interval arithmetic, playable below.

Guitar Craft plays in its own tuning, and for a while even that was close-held —
Tamm noted{' '}

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  "a measure of actual secrecy concerning specific guitar exercises and such things as his
  'new standard tuning' (which he has since publicly revealed)"
</CitationLink>
. The reveal, when Tamm witnessed it at a seminar, was Fripp spelling it string by string,
ending:
<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  "In other words, perfect fifths upward from the low C, with a G on top"
</CitationLink>
. So: C, G, D, A, E — four perfect fifths, starting a major third *below* the guitar's
ordinary bottom E — and then G, a minor third above the E, where the pattern would demand
a high B. Why break the pattern at the top? Work the physics: a fifth above E4 is B4, and
a plain string tuned to B4 at guitar scale length sits at the edge of its breaking
tension. The G is the compromise the instrument itself insists on.
<EditorialMark label="our reasoning" />

The consequences are not opinions; they are interval arithmetic, checkable on any
fretboard. Stack fifths and the tuning hands you **quintal and quartal voicings**
as its default grips — open, wide, rooty — while the close, thirds-based triads
that fall naturally under the fingers in standard tuning become stretches. And{' '}

<CitationLink source="wikipedia-nst">
  because five of the six intervals between adjacent strings are identical, NST is a
  <strong>regular tuning</strong>: a shape moved across the string set keeps its sound
</CitationLink>
. Standard tuning's lone major third (G to B) breaks exactly that symmetry — the break
every guitarist meets as the chord shape that must be re-fingered. In NST there is mostly
one shape. The first recorded appearance, per

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  Tamm, is "possibly" "Maquillage" — "with its gutsy low C, a major third below the
  guitar's normal sixth-string E — here prominently displayed as the tonic"
</CitationLink>
.

<EditorialMark /> Now put this page next to [chapter 3.2](/machine/what-the-tape-does/). A
feedback loop punishes dense, close-voiced, fast-moving harmony and rewards open,
spectrally clean, slow-moving sound — and here is a tuning whose default grips are exactly
the open, quintal, non-functional voicings a long loop can survive. The resonance is ours
to point out, and we find it hard to unsee. **Whether Fripp connected the tuning to the
harmonic demands of loop playing is a question, not a claim** — nothing in the sources we
hold asserts it, and this page will not assert it either. It is put in front of you the
way the machine puts a loop in front of a phrase: see whether it locks.
