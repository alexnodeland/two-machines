# The Room

<EditorialMark /> No instrument on this page. The argument here is about what happens on
your side of the speaker, and prose can carry it — mostly Fripp's own prose, which needs
no help. The connections drawn between his statements are ours, and wear the mark where
they are drawn.

Asked in 1979 what he wanted from an audience, Fripp drew the distinction this whole
site is built to serve:{' '}

<CitationLink source="tooley-1979">
  "A person who listens exerts an act of attention. A person who hears is a passive
  listener, if you like. A passive hearer. It's the difference between being a human being
  and being a vegetable."
</CitationLink>
Listening is something you *do*. <EditorialMark label="ours" /> Every page here has been
an attempt to convert hearing into listening by giving your attention something to grip: a
number to count toward, a knob whose consequences you predicted, a return you waited for.
The instruments are attention machines before they are anything else.

The performances made the same demand in person. Fripp's alternative to the music
industry's arenas was what{' '}

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  Tamm calls "the Frippism par excellence of the late 1970s": the "small, mobile
  intelligent unit"
</CitationLink>
— one man, one guitar, two machines — and the 1979 tour took it to
<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  "small clubs, restaurants, art galleries, record shops, radio station studios, museums,
  record company offices, and even the occasional pizza parlor"
</CitationLink>
, under

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">the tour's motto, "human contact"</CitationLink>.
<EditorialMark label="ours" /> Nobody in a record shop at lunchtime is owed the hits.
Whatever attention arrived, arrived voluntarily — which is exactly the kind the music asks
for. It was as much an argument about the business as about sound: a performing economy
small enough to say yes quickly and mobile enough to leave.

And the machine's own aesthetic criterion, in Fripp's words, about wanting to walk
unannounced into another performer's set:{' '}

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  "What I should personally like to do is to add the random factor, the factor of hazard …
  having become familiar with the tonal center, improvise over the top of it"
</CitationLink>
. The factor of hazard. <EditorialMark label="ours" /> A tape loop is a hazard generator
you carry with you: every pass returns your own playing as a slightly decayed stranger,
and the performance is your response to it in front of witnesses. The rig does not
eliminate risk; it schedules it.

<EditorialMark label="ours" /> Attention, smallness, hazard. If the site's thesis is two
cycles realigning, its ethic is this page: be present enough to hear the return coming.
