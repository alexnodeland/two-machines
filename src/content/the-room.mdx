# The Room

<EditorialMark /> The last argument is about what happens on your side of the speaker, so
the instrument on this page is your attention — and the prose is mostly Fripp's own, which
needs no help.

Asked in 1979 what he wanted from an audience, Fripp drew the distinction this whole
site is built to serve:{' '}

<CitationLink source="tooley-1979">
  "A person who listens exerts an act of attention. A person who hears is a passive
  listener, if you like. A passive hearer. It's the difference between being a human being
  and being a vegetable."
</CitationLink>
Listening is something you *do*. <EditorialMark /> Hearing becomes listening the moment
your attention has something to grip: a number to count toward, a knob whose consequences
you predicted, a return you waited for. Every exercise you have played here was one of
those grips — build the same handholds into your own practice, and any loop becomes an
attention machine.

Fripp was still teaching that distinction in 2025, opening the Alexander Technique
Congress in Dublin. Per{' '}

<CitationLink source="dublin-keynote-notes">
  his own keynote notes, there are "three qualities of attention: active, passive, and
  none whatsoever"; the stakes are stated flat — "Where our attention is, is where we are.
  The quality of our attention is all we properly have in life" — and so is the good news:
  "active attention may be practised."
</CitationLink>

The performances made the same demand in person. Fripp's alternative to the music
industry's arenas was what{' '}

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  Tamm calls "the Frippism par excellence of the late 1970s": the "small, mobile
  intelligent unit"
</CitationLink>
— one man, one guitar, two machines. Under
<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">the tour's motto, "human contact"</CitationLink>, the
1979 tour took it to

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  "small clubs, restaurants, art galleries, record shops, radio station studios, museums,
  record company offices, and even the occasional pizza parlor."
</CitationLink>
<EditorialMark /> Nobody in a record shop at lunchtime is owed the hits. Whatever
attention arrived, arrived voluntarily — which is exactly the kind the music asks for. It
was as much an argument about the business as about sound: a performing economy small
enough to say yes quickly and mobile enough to leave.

And the machine's own aesthetic criterion, in Fripp's words, about wanting to walk
unannounced into another performer's set:{' '}

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  "What I should personally like to do is to add the random factor, the factor of hazard …
  having become familiar with the tonal center, improvise over the top of it."
</CitationLink>

The factor of hazard. <EditorialMark /> A tape loop is a hazard generator you carry with
you: every pass returns your own playing as a slightly decayed stranger, and the
performance is your response to it in front of witnesses. The rig does not eliminate risk;
it schedules it.

<EditorialMark /> Attention, smallness, hazard. Take all three to your own rig: play rooms
small enough for human contact, commit enough to the loop to be at risk, and be present
enough to hear the return coming.
