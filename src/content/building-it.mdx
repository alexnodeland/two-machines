# Building it

Four ways to own this instrument, from an afternoon's rearrangement of pedals you
already have to the real thing with reels. Concrete numbers throughout; the settings
are the ones the [rig on the front page](/) runs, so every tier can be rehearsed
there first. <EditorialMark /> The tiers are ordered by cost, not by legitimacy: the
system is the signal path, and all four have it — the table at the bottom is the
same performance in four vocabularies.

## Pedalboard

The minimum kit is famously short — in{' '}

<CitationLink source="lamont-1">
  Lamont's words, "any electric guitar, distortion FX, volume pedal, long, decaying
  delay."
</CitationLink>
That is the whole list. Set the delay long — the rig's range is 1.5 to 8 seconds, and 3 to
5 is where the idiom lives — with feedback around 0.75: audibly repeating, definitely
decaying. Unity is where accumulation begins; keep a hand near the knob.

Two translations from the two-machine world, both taught in
[the grammar](/machine/the-grammar/): a delay switched _off with trails engaged_ is
the record head lifted — the loop keeps sounding, nothing new enters — and input
gain ahead of the delay is the record pedal. If your looper has an independent
record control, you already own the two-pedal split.

## DAW or patch

Three tracks: a live input, a delay return with its feedback path, and a master.
The delay is the whole trick, and any environment exposes it. In{' '}

<CitationLink source="maxmsp-tapin">
  Max, a <code>tapin~</code>/<code>tapout~</code> pair
</CitationLink>
with a gain stage in the feedback loop is the two-machine system in five objects; in

<CitationLink source="puredata-delay">
  Pure Data, <code>delwrite~</code> and <code>vd~</code>, with a <code>line~</code> ramp
  on the delay time
</CitationLink>
so changes glide instead of clicking — the software equivalent of sliding a machine along
the bench. Keep the feedback gain _outside_ the delay object, where you can see it: that
knob is the playback machine's output level, and watching it is half the discipline.

## Code

A small implementation is not a metaphor for the system — it _is_ the system, and
one is running on this page's site right now: the rig is a
[quiver](https://github.com/alexnodeland/quiver) patch — delay line, rolloff,
saturation, feedback gain, limiter — compiled to WebAssembly, wired exactly as
[chapter 1](/machine/what-it-is/)'s diagram. The repository is open; the patch is a
few dozen lines. If you can route a signal graph, you can build this tier in an
evening, and you will understand the other three better for it.

## Tape

The real thing: two reel-to-reel machines, one reel of tape threaded across both,
the playback output cabled to the record input. Honestly: this is the expensive
tier, in money and in care — working machines, tape stock, splicing, and the
operational habits the other tiers simulate for free. Two details the simulations
hide. Starting the system means releasing both transports together, and keeping
them in sympathy is a physical skill. And the take-up reel is the recording — every
performance archives itself<EditorialMark /> — part of why so much of this music
survives at all. Schellinx's{' '}

<CitationLink source="sigsym-2">
  Sign &amp; Symptoms part 2 — "How did it work?"
</CitationLink>
is the modern field report: he rebuilt the rig with real machines and wrote down
everything that fought back.
