# Where it came from

Fripp did not invent the two-machine delay, has never claimed to, and neither did
Eno. The idea is older than either of them and it arrived, as these things do, more
than once. Harold Schellinx put the right frame on it:{' '}

<CitationLink source="sigsym-1">
  "it doesn't make a lot of sense to point out some one … as the 'originator(s)' of the
  idea … it's pretty much something that came along with the machine."
</CitationLink>

Once tape recorders had separate record and playback heads, the gap between the heads
_was_ a delay, and running one tape between two whole machines only made the gap long
enough to compose with. What follows is not a chain of invention but a list of independent
arrivals.

**Paris, 1953.** Jacques Poullin builds the morphophone at the GRM:{' '}

<CitationLink source="scorranese-2025">
  a tape loop on a rotating wheel, read by one record head and ten repositionable playback
  heads, each with its own filter.
</CitationLink>

A multi-tap tape delay built as a piece of furniture, in use for musique concrète a
full generation before the rock lineage noticed.

**New York, 1961.** Tony Conrad writes{' '}

<CitationLink source="akg-conrad">
  <em>Three Loops for Performers and Tape Recorders</em> — the score is dated 8–21
  November 1961.
</CitationLink>

Its appendix diagrams the exact rig this site models:

<CitationLink source="sigsym-1">
  "two tape recorders are used, arranged so that one plays what has been recorded some
  interval of time previously by the other."
</CitationLink>

Written down, as a score, two years before anyone gave the technique a name.

**Paris, 1963.** Terry Riley, scoring Ken Dewey's play _The Gift_, strings one tape
between two recorders and names the rig the Time Lag Accumulator:{' '}

<CitationLink source="ircam-riley">
  "connect two tape recorders. The first one is playing back, the second recording, the
  tape stretched across the heads of both."
</CitationLink>

The name is a plain description — the machine accumulates time-lagged copies — and
[chapter 3](/machine/what-the-tape-does/) leans on exactly that accuracy.

**Toronto, 1966.** Pauline Oliveros, in her own words:{' '}

<CitationLink source="oliveros-econtact">
  "I started placing a reel on one machine then running the tape over to another machine
  so the tape would go past two playback heads."
</CitationLink>

That system is the one behind _I of IV_ — recorded in real time as a four-channel,
two-machine delay. A complete, performing practice, a decade before the records this
site orbits.

**London, 1972.** Eno sets up two Revoxes in his home studio and, per{' '}

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  Tamm, shows Fripp "the tape-loop system" that Fripp would take up as his own five years
  later.
</CitationLink>
That

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  September 1972 evening produced "The Heavenly Music Corporation," which became Side One
  of their <em>(No Pussyfooting)</em>
</CitationLink>
— the first document of guitar meeting the rig. Fripp's own account of that evening:

<CitationLink source="tooley-1979">
  "He didn't tell me how it worked. I simply intuited what was happening, and that was
  side one of No Pussyfooting."
</CitationLink>

**New York, 1977–78.** Per{' '}

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  Tamm, Fripp's "own work" with the system begins in June 1977
</CitationLink>
and the name arrives at

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  the Kitchen, 5 February 1978 — his first official solo appearance in over three years,
  and "the first time he used the name 'Frippertronics' for his tape-delay system."
</CitationLink>
The concert itself was an accident of scale:

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  per Tamm it had been planned as an intimate performance for invited friends in the poet
  Joanna Walton's apartment, and moved when they feared it would get too noisy for the
  room.
</CitationLink>
Both dates rest on Tamm.

The chain, then: a French laboratory, an American score, a named rig in a Paris
theatre piece, a Toronto studio practice, an English home studio — and then a
guitarist who took the machine on tour under a name of his own coinage and used it
nightly, in public, for years. That use is what the rest of this site examines.
