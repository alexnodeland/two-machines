# The grammar

<EditorialMark /> Chapter 3 said what the tape does; this page is only *how to play it*.
Six habits, each one a consequence of the machine's constraints, each one practicable
right here with no guitar and no microphone — every lesson deep-links the rig on the front
page with its settings loaded, and the tone pad is instrument enough. Calling these six
the grammar of the idiom is our curriculum, not anyone's official syllabus.

## Lesson 1 · Three notes and silence

[Open the rig with this lesson's settings](/?preset=three-notes) — 3.5 seconds of
tape, playback at 0.80.

Seed the loop with exactly three tones, then stop.{' '}

<CitationLink source="lamont-1">
  Lamont's grammar for choosing them: the first "becomes the root note of the key of the
  piece"; the second is "often either the fifth, or the third"; the third, "something less
  obvious – a higher octave, a second or a major seventh"
</CitationLink>
. Then the real exercise: sit with the loop until it suggests the next move, and only then
play it. The silence is not a pause in the lesson. It is the lesson.

<ThreeNotes />

## Lesson 2 · Beeping and droning

[Open the rig](/?preset=beeping) — 4.0 seconds, playback at 0.78.

The idiom has two elementary textures. Short, separated marks stay legible as
rhythm — beeping. Held tones fuse into standing harmony — droning. Tap the pads
briefly, then hold one, and listen for the moment one becomes the other: it is a
property of your note length against the loop, not of the notes themselves. A whole
performance can be built from deciding, moment by moment, which of the two you are
doing.

<BeepingDroning />

One revolution of the ring is one pass of the tape. Notes land where you played
them and fade a little each time round, and the meter under it names how full the
loop already is — watch it when lesson 4 sends you back here.

## Lesson 3 · Not committing

[Open the rig](/?preset=not-committing) — record head at **zero**, monitor up.

<NotCommitting />

Play. The room hears you; the tape ignores you. Nothing you do accumulates — check
the readout, watch the loop stay empty — and the moment you raise the record head,
you are composing again. This is the two-pedal split from chapter 3.3 as a hand
skill, and it is the most important technique on this site: it means committing to
the loop is always a choice, never a side effect of playing.

<EditorialMark label="the same idea" /> Every modern looper has this lesson hiding in it.
Delay off with trails engaged *is* lifting the record head. A record-enable footswitch
*is* the record pedal. Input gain before the loop *is* the same control in a different
housing. We teach them as one idea deliberately — learn the split once and every rig you
ever meet already has it. No published method teaches this exercise; it is built directly
from the two-machine wiring, and this page says so rather than borrowing authority it does
not have.

## Lesson 4 · Avoiding mud

[Open the rig](/?preset=mud) — playback at 0.96, record head full: deliberately too
much.

Fill it. Play fast, play thick, and get what{' '}

<CitationLink source="lamont-2">
  Lamont watched every newcomer make: "the sonic equivalent of mud – a thick and
  unappealing mess"
</CitationLink>
. This is failure on purpose, and it is the fastest way to learn where the boundary is.
Now pull the playback level down and thin your playing —
<CitationLink source="lamont-2">
  "too many notes in the same octave tend to sound muddy"
</CitationLink>
, so spread out — and listen to the loop clear itself over the next passes. Mud is not a
wrong answer; it is a boundary you learn by crossing it in both directions.

<AvoidingMud />

## Lesson 5 · Volume swells and soft attack

[Open the rig](/?preset=swells) — 5.0 seconds, monitor low.

Every attack you commit to the loop returns every pass, forever. One hard transient
is a metronome you did not mean to start. So enter notes without one: swell in from
silence, let the tone arrive already sustaining. This is chapter 3.3's
sustain-governs-gesture claim moved into the hands — the machine rewards the note
that begins as if it had always been there.

<Swells />

## Lesson 6 · Writing a structure

[Open the rig](/?preset=sequence-plan) — 5.0 seconds, playback at 0.87: room for a
drone to live.

Before touching anything, write a plan. The format is Lamont's, and his own worked
example is worth quoting whole:{' '}

<CitationLink source="lamont-1">
  "Set up organ drone in D / Sprinkle some high glockenspiel notes randomly in the loop /
  Guitar improv in Dm / When organ drone has faded change chord to A or Em, let it cycle /
  Return organ drone to D / Guitar improv in D major but fed into looper so very few
  notes"
</CitationLink>
. The form is fixed; the content is free. Write your own — five or six lines, keys and
events, nothing more — and play it against the rig. Whether Fripp ever practised from
anything like a written plan we do not know; this is a method for you, not a biography of
him.
