# The interlock

<EditorialMark /> One claim organises this whole chapter, and it is ours: **the clapping
exercise and the *Discipline* interlock are the same object at different scales.** A room
counting five against seven and two guitarists cycling fifteen against fourteen are
running the same arithmetic — one shared pulse, unequal whole-number cycles, a guaranteed
return at the least common multiple, and a long interlock in between where nothing agrees.
You played the small version in [Part I](/two-cycles/). This page scales it up.

A guarantee, printed where it belongs: **the presets below carry cycle lengths and
downbeats only.** A meter is public analytical fact, the kind of thing a review
prints; a pattern of hits is a composition, and none is reproduced here. Anything
beyond the downbeat is yours to build in the rack.

## The numbers, from the players

The cycles are not fan lore. Asked in 1981 whether "Discipline" was in five, Fripp
answered:{' '}

<CitationLink source="fall-1981">
  "Basically, it breaks down that the rhythm section is in seventeen, and the front line
  are in fifteen. And it varies a bit along the way"
</CitationLink>
. The drummer's leg of the claim is independent and precise:
<CitationLink source="drum-2012">
  Bruford's groove "comprises 17 sixteenth-notes — which would make the time signature
  17/16 — tethered to quarter-notes on the bass drum"
</CitationLink>
, and he demonstrates it himself in
<CitationLink source="bruford-beat-1982">*Bruford and the Beat* (1982)</CitationLink>. The
second guitar's fourteen is the one part of the received account without an independent
leg — it circulates from a single tradition, with
<CitationLink source="garone-2025">
  Garone's practical reconstruction ("Adrian plays a loop in 15/16 … Robert plays a
  similar phrase in 14/16, cutting off the last note")
</CitationLink>
as corroboration — so treat the 14 as well-attested rather than proven, and note that
Fripp's own "it varies a bit along the way" is the honest footing for the whole table. The
generative device in Garone's description is worth staring at: one player takes the
other's phrase and cuts the last note. That single edit is what turns unison into an
interlock.

Load the guitars-only pair — 15 against 14 — in the engine above and let it run:
**the two guitars come home every 210 sixteenths**, about forty-eight seconds at
the demonstration tempo. Add the seventeen and all three parts agree only once
every **3,570 pulses** — around thirteen and a half minutes, longer than the track.
A listener never hears the full resolution, and that is not a flaw; it is the
composition. (Tempo honesty, as in Part I: on record these sixteenths run near 480
to the minute — too fast to hear the interlock _as_ an interlock, which is exactly
why the presets slow down.)

## Not phasing

Say it loudly: **this is not Reich phasing.** In _Piano Phase_ two players start
identical and one accelerates — different tempos, continuous drift, no exact
return: Part I's _drift_ object. The _Discipline_ interlock is the _offset_ object:
one shared pulse kept strictly, cycles of different lengths, and a return you can
print the bar number of. They sound superficially alike — repeating cells sliding
past each other — and they are structurally opposite. The grid view above draws for
one and refuses for the other; that refusal is the distinction.

## Circulations

Guitar Craft has a group practice called circulation: players sit in a circle and
pass notes around it — each plays one note of a line in turn, so the melody exists
only in the whole room. <EditorialMark label="ours" /> Seen from this site's angle,
a circulation is a delay line built out of people: the circle is the loop, and one
seat's worth of waiting is the delay time. What the tape does with a span of
oxide, the ensemble does with attention. We keep the description deliberately
thin here — the practice deserves its sources, and the authorised text is on its
way to this desk; the analogy, meanwhile, is marked as what it is.

The room that claps five against seven, the circle that passes a note, the two
guitars a sixteenth apart, the tape running between two machines: four sizes of
one object. That is the Discipline half's claim, and by now you can check every
number in it yourself.
