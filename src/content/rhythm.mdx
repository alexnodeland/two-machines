# The interlock

<EditorialMark /> One claim organises this whole chapter: **the clapping exercise and the
*Discipline* interlock are the same object at different scales.** A room counting five
against seven and two guitarists cycling fifteen against fourteen are running the same
arithmetic — one shared pulse, unequal whole-number cycles, a guaranteed return at the
least common multiple, and a long interlock in between where nothing agrees. You played
the small version in [Part I](/two-cycles/). This page scales it up.

The room counting five against seven has a name in the teaching. Per{' '}

<CitationLink source="dublin-keynote-notes">
  Fripp's notes to his 2025 Dublin keynote: "This simple exercise of playing in two
  groups, one in 5 and the other in 7, is called THRAKKING in Guitar Craft. It was
  initially introduced as an exercise of health and vigour, and a binding exercise to
  bring together participants on Guitar Craft courses."
</CitationLink>

The presets below carry cycle lengths and downbeats only — anything beyond the
downbeat is yours to build in the rack ([the full guarantee](/cycles/) lives with
the engine).

Press play and let it run — do not count. Watch the pulse readouts instead: the
return tells you when all three cycles agree again, and the longest interlock names
the stretch where nothing does. Listening through one full disagreement is the
exercise.

## The numbers, from the players

The cycles are not fan lore. Asked in 1981 whether "Discipline" was in five, Fripp
answered:{' '}

<CitationLink source="fall-1981">
  "Basically, it breaks down that the rhythm section is in seventeen, and the front line
  are in fifteen. And it varies a bit along the way."
</CitationLink>

The drummer's leg of the claim is independent and precise:{' '}

<CitationLink source="drum-2012">
  Bruford's groove "comprises 17 sixteenth-notes — which would make the time signature
  17/16 — tethered to quarter-notes on the bass drum."
</CitationLink>

He demonstrates it himself in{' '}

<CitationLink source="bruford-and-the-beat">Bruford and the Beat (1982)</CitationLink>.
The second guitar's count is usually given as fourteen, per

<CitationLink source="7dmedia-interlock">
  Garone's practical reconstruction: "Adrian plays a loop in 15/16 … Robert plays a
  similar phrase in 14/16, cutting off the last note."
</CitationLink>

— load your own count in the rack above and test it; Fripp's "it varies a bit
along the way" is your licence. The generative device in Garone's description is
worth staring at: one player takes the other's phrase and cuts the last note. That
single edit is what turns unison into an interlock.

Load the guitars-only pair — 15 against 14 — in the engine above and let it run:
**the two guitars come home every 210 sixteenths**, about forty-eight seconds at
the demonstration tempo. Add the seventeen and all three parts agree only once
every **3,570 pulses** — around thirteen and a half minutes, longer than the track.
A listener never hears the full resolution, and that is not a flaw; it is the
composition. (Tempo honesty, as in Part I: on record these sixteenths run near 480
to the minute — too fast to hear the interlock _as_ an interlock, which is exactly
why the presets slow down.)

## Not phasing

If you know _Piano Phase_, head off the obvious comparison: **this is not Reich
phasing.** In{' '}

<CitationLink source="7dmedia-interlock">
  <em>Piano Phase</em>, two players start identical and one accelerates — the same
  contrast Garone draws when reconstructing the interlock
</CitationLink>
— different tempos, continuous drift, no exact return: Part I's _drift_ object. The
_Discipline_ interlock is the _offset_ object: one shared pulse kept strictly, cycles of
different lengths, and a return you can print the bar number of. They sound superficially
alike — repeating cells sliding past each other — and they are structurally opposite. The
grid view above draws for one and refuses for the other; that refusal is the distinction.

## Circulations

Guitar Craft has{' '}

<CitationLink source="wikipedia-guitar-craft">
  a group practice called circulation: players sit in a circle and pass notes around it,
  each playing one note of a line in turn
</CitationLink>
— so the melody exists only in the whole room. <EditorialMark /> A circulation is a delay
line built out of people: the circle is the loop, and one seat's worth of waiting is the
delay time. What the tape does with a span of oxide, the ensemble does with attention.

The room that claps five against seven, the circle that passes a note, the two
guitars a sixteenth apart, the tape running between two machines: four sizes of
one object. That is the Discipline half's claim, and by now you can check every
number in it yourself.
