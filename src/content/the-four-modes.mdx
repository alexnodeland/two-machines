# The four modes

<EditorialMark /> Fripp gave the system a name, and then a taxonomy. The names sound like
jokes — partly because they are; the silliness was chosen — but each one marks a real,
technical difference: **what changes is the signal path and what sits inside it.** The
machine is the constant. Read each mode below as a patching instruction — what joins the
loop, and what the loop is for — and every one of them is reachable from your own rig.

The dates, for orientation. The naming came late — five years after the two-machine
system appeared on the Fripp & Eno records:

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  "it was during June of that year [1977] that what Fripp has called his 'own work' with
  the tape-loop-delay system, or Frippertronics, began."
</CitationLink>

The name arrived later still:

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  "On Sunday, February 5, 1978, Fripp made his first official solo appearance in over
  three years, at the Kitchen in Soho: this was also the first time he used the name
  'Frippertronics' for his tape-delay system"
</CitationLink>

The collaborations with Eno used the same two machines, but Fripp dates
_Frippertronics_ — his own work — from 1977. The formal definition he settled on in 1980
names the parts exactly: "that musical experience resulting at the interstice of Robert
Fripp and a small, mobile and appropriate level of technology, vis. his guitar,
Frippelboard [effects pedal board] and two Revoxes [reel-to-reel tape recorders]" —

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">quoted in Tamm</CitationLink>.

## Pure

The loops are the whole performance: guitar, the two machines, nothing else in the
path, and the accumulating tape is the piece. This is{' '}

<CitationLink source="tooley-1979">
  the 1979 tour — record shops, offices, unconventional rooms by design, as Fripp laid out
  at length that August
</CitationLink>
— and the recorded document is _Let the Power Fall_. What changes relative to the diagram
in [chapter 1](/machine/what-it-is/): nothing. Pure mode _is_ the diagram.

## Applied

The loops become one voice inside an ensemble recording: the signal path gains a
mixing desk, other musicians, a song. Per{' '}

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  Tamm, "At the time of its making, Sacred Songs represented the first recorded use of
  Frippertronics."
</CitationLink>
That is the Daryl Hall album Fripp produced in late 1977 (the label held it back; it was
released in 1980); Tamm singles out the Side One suite "Babs and Babs — Urban Landscape —
NYCNY". _Exposure_ is the same mode on Fripp's own record — and the E minor/G minor
collision from [chapter 3](/machine/what-the-tape-does/) happened here, applied
Frippertronics over a drum break.

## Discotronics

The loops meet a rhythm section built for dancing. Fripp defined it on the sleeve
itself, with the same word again — per{' '}

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  the <em>Under Heavy Manners</em> liner note quoted in Tamm, "Discotronics is defined as
  that musical experience resulting at the interstice of Frippertronics and disco."
</CitationLink>
What changes: the loop no longer sets the clock. A drummer does, and the tape's fixed
cycle runs against the band's — Part I's two-cycles object, staged inside a single piece.

## Soundscapes

The digital successor,{' '}

<CitationLink source="dgm-frippertronics">
  from the 1990s onward: the two Revoxes replaced by digital delay and loopers, the mono
  span of tape by long, layered, often emphatically stereo sustain.
</CitationLink>
The name changed with the hardware, which is the taxonomy's own logic — a different
machine in the path earns a different name. The tape era this site models ends here.
<EditorialMark /> What survives across the boundary is the practice: commit, listen, add,
wait.

<EditorialMark /> Every mode is defined by what meets what. The constant on one side is
always the same — a guitarist, a delay, and time — so when you change what sits on the
other side of your own loop, you are moving through this taxonomy whether you name the
modes or not.
