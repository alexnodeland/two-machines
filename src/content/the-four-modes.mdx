# The four modes

Fripp gave the system a name, and then a taxonomy. The names sound like jokes —
partly because they are; the silliness was chosen — but each one marks a real,
technical difference: **what changes is the signal path and what sits inside it.**
The machine is the constant. Timeline first, because the naming came surprisingly
late:{' '}

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  "it was during June of that year [1977] that what Fripp has called his 'own work' with
  the tape-loop-delay system, or Frippertronics, began"
</CitationLink>
— five years after the two-machine system itself appeared on the Fripp & Eno records. The
name arrived later still:
<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  "On Sunday, February 5, 1978, Fripp made his first official solo appearance in over
  three years, at the Kitchen in Soho: this was also the first time he used the name
  'Frippertronics' for his tape-delay system"
</CitationLink>
— a date for which Tamm is, so far, our sole authority. That gap in the timeline is itself
the first distinction worth drawing: the collaborations with Eno used the same two
machines, but Fripp dates *Frippertronics* — his own work — from 1977. The formal
definition he settled on in 1980 names the parts exactly: "that musical experience
resulting at the interstice of Robert Fripp and a small, mobile and appropriate level of
technology, vis. his guitar, Frippelboard [effects pedal board] and two Revoxes
[reel-to-reel tape recorders]" —
<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">quoted in Tamm</CitationLink>. There is that word
*interstice* again, doing its precise work.

## Pure

The loops are the whole performance: guitar, the two machines, nothing else in the
path, and the accumulating tape is the piece. This is the 1979 tour — record shops,
offices, a pizzeria — and the recorded document is _Let the Power Fall_. What
changes relative to the diagram in [chapter 1](/machine/what-it-is/): nothing. Pure
mode _is_ the diagram.

## Applied

The loops become one voice inside an ensemble recording: the signal path gains a
mixing desk, other musicians, a song. Per{' '}

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  Tamm — and the claim rests on him alone — "At the time of its making, Sacred Songs
  represented the first recorded use of Frippertronics"
</CitationLink>
, on the Daryl Hall album Fripp produced in 1977 (released, after a fight, in 1979); he
singles out the Side One suite "Babs and Babs — Urban Landscape — NYCNY". *Exposure* is
the same mode on Fripp's own record — and the E minor/G minor collision from [chapter
3](/machine/what-the-tape-does/) happened here, applied Frippertronics over a drum break.

## Discotronics

The loops meet a rhythm section built for dancing. Fripp defined it on the sleeve
itself, with the same word again: "Discotronics is defined as that musical
experience resulting at the interstice of Frippertronics and disco" —{' '}

<CitationLink source="tamm-1990">
  the *Under Heavy Manners* liner note, quoted in Tamm
</CitationLink>
. What changes: the loop no longer sets the clock. A drummer does, and the tape's fixed
cycle runs against the band's — Part I's two-cycles object, staged inside a single piece.

## Soundscapes

The digital successor, from the 1990s onward: the two Revoxes replaced by digital
delay and loopers, the mono span of tape by long, layered, often emphatically stereo
sustain. The name changed with the hardware, which is the taxonomy's own logic — a
different machine in the path earns a different name. The tape era this site models
ends here; what survives across the boundary is the practice: commit, listen, add,
wait.

One habit of the naming is worth noticing before moving on: every mode is defined by
its _interstice_ — what meets what. The constant on one side is always the same: a
guitarist, a delay, and time.
