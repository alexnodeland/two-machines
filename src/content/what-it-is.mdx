# What it is

Two reel-to-reel tape recorders sit side by side, and one reel of tape runs between
them: off the first machine's supply reel, past its heads, across the gap, past the
second machine's heads, onto the second machine's take-up reel. Machine one records;
machine two plays back; and machine two's output is wired back into machine one's
input, so everything played back is recorded again. The span of tape between the two
machines is the delay — a time you could measure with a ruler.

That is the whole mechanism, and it is the one drawn on{' '}

<CitationLink source="eno-discreet-music-sleeve">
  the record sleeve where most people first met it — the operational diagram on the jacket
  of Eno's <em>Discreet Music</em>
</CitationLink>
:

<DiscreetSchematic />

Asked to explain it plainly, Fripp did, in{' '}

<CitationLink source="gaskin-1979">
  1979: "I record on the left machine, the guitar is recorded on the left machine, the
  signal passes along the tape to the right machine where it's played back to the left
  machine and recorded a second time."
</CitationLink>
And so on: the second recording travels the same span, is played back a second time,
recorded a third. Each pass is another few seconds later and a little softer, and the
music accumulates.

Look closely at the right-hand edge of the diagram, because a subtlety lives there
that the word "loop" hides. **The tape is not a loop.** It runs from one machine to
the other and then winds onto the take-up reel — it never comes back. What returns is
the _signal_, carried by the wire from machine two's output to machine one's input.
The system behaves like a loop only because electricity closes the circle that the
tape does not; and, as{' '}

<CitationLink source="eno-discreet-music-sleeve">
  the sleeve notes in passing ("output stored on master tape")
</CitationLink>
{', '}the take-up reel is quietly performing a second job — the performance is being
stored as it happens. The recording is a by-product of the mechanism, not a separate act.

One more detail from the same{' '}

<CitationLink source="gaskin-1979">
  interview. Asked at what point the sound is released into the room: "Oh, straightaway.
  Unless, what I could do if I wanted to be crafty, would be to build up a chord which no
  one could hear and then turn the chord on."
</CitationLink>
Everything is live, by default — but the machine permits a performance the audience cannot
hear until it is revealed. Hold that thought; it becomes a whole lesson in [the
grammar](/machine/the-grammar/).

The [rig on the front page](/) is this diagram, playable: drag machine two to set
the span, and the readout gives it to you in centimetres and seconds at once. As
this page loads, your machines stand <DelayReadout /> apart — the same number the
rig is using right now, and a time you could measure with a ruler.

<EditorialMark label="reasoning" /> Why does the span sit at three to five seconds? Drag
the machines close and the return arrives while you are still inside your phrase — an
echo, colouring what you play. Push past about three seconds and the return lands after
the phrase has ended: not an effect any more but an answer, something you can play
*against*. Much further apart and you start forgetting what you committed before it comes
back. Three to five seconds is the window where the tape stops decorating you and becomes
the other musician — and it happens to be about the span two decks on a bench naturally
gave. Drag the rig to both extremes and listen for where the change happens for you.
