# What the tape does to your music

<EditorialMark /> The delay line is not a neutral effect. The style everyone recognises as
Frippertronics — modal, slow-moving, sustained, built on pedal points — is not a taste
that happens to pass through a tape machine. It is an adaptation to what the machine does,
and this page says what that is — one consequence per musical dimension.

One fact drives everything below: **the system is additive-only.** Whatever reaches
the tape joins what is already circulating, and nothing can be taken back out. You
cannot erase; you can only wait for decay. Hold that fact and the three sections that
follow are consequences, one per musical dimension.

## 3.1 Rhythm <EditorialMark label="a perpetual canon" />

Everything you play returns after the span of tape — call it _T_ seconds — at pitch,
at level set by the playback machine, forever. That is the definition of a canon
whose imitation interval is fixed by hardware. If your phrase lasts exactly _T_, or
divides it evenly, each return lands on itself: a phase-locked round. If it does not,
each return lands somewhere new against your playing, and the texture accumulates.

This is not new arithmetic. It is [Part I](/two-cycles/)'s distinction wearing tape:
a phrase that divides _T_ is the offset family and comes home; a phrase that ignores
_T_ drifts against a cycle that never negotiates. Neither is failure — the locked
canon and the accumulating wash are the two idioms the machine offers — but you
choose between them with your phrase length, whether you meant to or not.

Both faders step in quarter seconds, so every ratio below is rational and the
return time is exact. Play the four-note figure and choose your idiom:

Listen for where the returns land. Stacked on the phrase's own starts, each pass
reinforcing the last, is the locked canon; scattered somewhere new each pass,
thickening instead of repeating, is the wash accumulating. One fader moved a
quarter second is the whole difference between the two idioms.

## 3.2 Harmony <EditorialMark label="the feedback knob is the harmonic-rhythm control" />

Because the system is additive-only, a chord change is not a change: it is an
addition, laid over everything the tape still remembers. Move from E minor to
anything before E minor has decayed, and both sound at once. The rate at which you
may change harmony is therefore bounded by decay time — and decay time is the
playback level. Turn the knob up and your harmony must move more slowly; near unity
it may hardly move at all. That is why this repertoire lives on modes and pedal
points: not a stylistic preference, but the set of harmonic behaviours a long memory
can survive. Test the bound yourself:
[open the rig with four seconds of tape at playback 0.95](/?d=4&fb=0.95), seed one
chord, and try to move to a second before the first has faded — the knob, not your
taste, is setting how fast your harmony may change.

Fripp met the collision on purpose. Describing the applied Frippertronics on
_Sacred Songs_, over a drum break in a Daryl Hall composition, he recalled{' '}

<CitationLink source="tooley-1979">
  one moment where "a Frippertronics loop in E minor and a Frippertronics loop in G minor
  collided beautifully. An exquisite interstice."
</CitationLink>
Two keys at once, chosen and kept. And a contemporary reviewer describing a 1979
performance reported him working not in chords but in accumulated
<CitationLink source="michigan-daily-1979">
  harmonic overtones — high frequencies sent through the loop, repeating for short periods
  — then varying the tonal field
</CitationLink>
— spectral thinking, building a sound rather than a progression.

## 3.3 Melody <EditorialMark label="sustain governs gesture" />

Play a fast line into a long loop and every note of it returns as harmony against
everything you play next. Eight notes a second for four seconds is thirty-two pitches
circulating at once: mud, and the machine did exactly what you asked. Slow, sustained
gestures survive because each addition has room to mean something before the next
arrives. The tape does not forbid virtuosity; it prices it.

Mud here is not failure — grammar lesson 4 crosses this boundary on purpose. It is
the machine pricing the fast line.

**The tape enforces a hard split between harmony
and melody: what you commit to the loop becomes harmony, and what you play over it
stays melody.** The two-volume-pedal technique — one pedal deciding how much of you
goes onto the tape, the other how much of you the room hears now — is the mechanism
of that split. With the record pedal down and the monitor up, you are soloing over
your own accumulated harmony while committing nothing. The pedals are not a mixing
convenience; they are the instrument's answer to the one fact this page began with.
You cannot take anything back out of the loop — so the technique gives you total
control over what goes in.

<EditorialMark /> That is the whole adaptation: rhythm chosen by phrase length, harmony
paced by decay, melody priced by sustain. Play against the machine for ten minutes and you
will start making the same choices the idiom made — not because they are Fripp's, but
because the tape prices everything else.
