# Two cycles

<EditorialMark /> One claim carries this page. Everything else on it is arithmetic you can
check by counting.

Start with a shared pulse. Two people clap the same steady beat: nothing interesting
is happening yet, but the foundation is laid — there is a common clock, and
everything below stands on it.

Now give them different cycle lengths. One person counts five and claps on one and
four. The other counts seven and claps on one, four and six. Their downbeats
immediately wander apart. Play it before reading another word:

<Cycles preset="claps" />

Count along with the grid and two things happen. First, at pulse **35** — five
sevens, seven fives — the downbeats land together again. Not roughly: exactly, and
provably, because 35 is the least common multiple of 5 and 7. The return is not a
feeling, it is arithmetic. Second, on the way there the two patterns pass through a
stretch of **17 consecutive pulses on which they agree about nothing**. That is the
interlock — the length of time you are on your own — and you will meet that
seventeen again.

The return is the event. When a room full of people claps this exercise, the moment
the downbeats realign is audible and felt; the room lands together, and everyone
knows it without being told. The same object appears at a larger scale when two
guitars play interlocking cycles over a drum cycle of seventeen — fifteen sixteenths
for one guitar by Fripp's own account, and fourteen for the other by the usual
count (load any count you prefer below and test it):

<Cycles preset="discipline" />

The two guitars come home every **210** sixteenths — about twenty-nine seconds at
the preset's tempo. All three parts agree only once every **3,570** — roughly eight
minutes at this tempo, far longer than any listener sits inside the groove. A
listener never hears the full resolution. (The preset runs just under the record's
clip — fast enough that the body can feel the meter, still slow enough to hear the
interlock as an interlock. The engine carries cycle lengths and downbeats only;
[the full statement](/cycles/) lives with it.)

One distinction carries everything that follows. There are **two different ways for cycles to
fail to line up**, and they are not the same object, however alike they sound.

**Offset** is what you have been playing: one shared pulse, different whole-number
cycle lengths. Realignment is guaranteed, at the least common multiple. It might take
35 pulses or 3,570, but the return is coming, and you can print the pulse on which it
arrives.

**Drift** is something else: two independent tempos. Run the same pattern against
itself with one copy four per cent fast, and no arithmetic will bring the downbeats
back — the two hands just sweep through every possible phase relationship, forever:

<Cycles preset="drift" />

Notice the grid view refuses to draw in drift. That is not a missing feature. A grid
is a promise that there is one shared lattice of pulses to draw on, and in drift
there is no such lattice — nothing to come home to. The only honest picture is two
dials turning at their own speeds, separating.

Keep the two objects apart. When any texture repeats around you, the first question
to ask is which one you are inside: is a return coming, or not?

<EditorialMark /> Here is the bridge — Fripp never put it this way: **a tape delay is a
fixed cycle running against your phrase length.** The span of tape between two machines
repeats whatever you give it every few seconds, exactly, forever. If your phrase divides
that span, you are in the offset family — a canon that locks, a return you can count on.
If it does not, your playing drifts against a cycle that never negotiates, and every pass
lands somewhere new. The rig at the top of the front page is one of these two objects at
every moment, and which one it is depends entirely on how you play against it.

The rest of the site is that sentence, taken seriously in both directions. **The
Machine** follows it into the tape: the rig, where it came from, and what a fixed
cycle does to the music played into it. **The Discipline** follows it into the
hands: the interlock, the tuning, the line — and, as an appendix, where the
numbers come from.
