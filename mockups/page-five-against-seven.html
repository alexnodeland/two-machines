A counting exercise · Robert Fripp

Count to five. Clap on one and four. Count to seven. Clap on one, four and six. Now run both at once.

One shared pulse, two cycle lengths. The claps drift apart, interlock, and land back together on the same beat only once every thirty-five.

Why it takes thirty-five beats to come home

Lay the pulse out five to a row. The five-pattern goes rigid: every clap falls in column one or column four, row after row, straight down the page.

The seven-pattern can't sit still. Each of its claps lands one row down and two columns right, wrapping when it runs off the edge — so it leans across the grid as a set of slants. Only after seven rows does it arrive back at the top-left corner where it began.

Five rows of seven, seven rows of five: thirty-five. That's the whole cycle, and where the two coincide the beat turns white.

  • 14 claps in the five
  • 15 claps in the seven
  • 6 beats where both hands fall together
  • 17 beats of pure interlock after beat 11 — the longest stretch with no agreement at all

Running it with a group

Nobody speeds up to meet the other side.

  1. Set one pulse

    Everyone counts out loud on the same beat. Speed doesn't matter. Evenness does — the exercise dies the moment the pulse bends.

  2. Learn the five

    One, two, three, four, five. Clap on one and four. Stay in it until the clapping stops costing you attention.

  3. Learn the seven

    One through seven, clapping on one, four and six. Note the shorter gap between four and six — that's the part that trips people.

  4. Split the room

    Half hold the five, half take the seven. Both keep counting their own number and ignore the other side. Wait for beat one to come back around.

On the name

There's no fixed term in Fripp's teaching along the lines of positive and negative five. What's more likely being remembered is this exercise.

In a 2025 talk to the Alexander Technique Congress, he had the audience count in five and clap on one and four, then set a pattern in seven against it — clapping on one, four and six — and finally had different groups run the five and the seven at the same time.
